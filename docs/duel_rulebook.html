Duel

A standoff for two badges and two players. The badges count you in, both rings go blue, and the first one to move loses.

menu Duel badges 2 apart 2–4 m radio badge to badge sound optional firmware 1.0.44 board rev F as of 23 Nov 2026
The idea

Your badge judges them

Two players face each other, each with a badge pointed at the other player. When both are set, the badges agree a countdown between themselves. Then both rings turn solid blue and nobody may move. It can last two seconds or two minutes: there is no time limit, only nerve.

Each badge watches the person opposite, never its own player. The badge can't feel itself move; what its radar sees is the other player. So the call is always made by your opponent's equipment, and no badge ever declares its own player the loser. The badge that sees the move tells the other one, and both show the result.

Duel needs two badges. With one, the Sneak rulebook has the game for a whole room.

Before the first round

Setting up

  1. Both players pick Games → Duel.

    Until it hears the other badge, the screen says no other badge yet. Then it shows finding and the other badge's name, and once the two have picked each other, vs and the name. Check it's the badge you meant to play. No Wi-Fi needed: the badges talk directly to each other.

  2. Stand 2 to 4 metres apart, facing each other.

    Each badge ignores anyone further than 5 m, and sees 60° either side of straight ahead. Closer than about 2 m, a small lean is a big move to the radar.

  3. Aim your badge at the other player, and keep it still.

    Best of all, stand each badge upright on a table, chair or stand just in front of its player, facing the opponent. If you hold it, hold it flat against your chest with both hands. A badge swinging on a lanyard is not fair play — see Fair play.

  4. Clear the space between you.

    Spectators stand behind the players or well off to the side, not in between.

  5. Press SELECT, or touch the bullseye, when you're set.

    The screen says you are set. Press again to take it back. The round starts the moment both players are set.

One round

Ready, steady, hold

The countdown is a different random length every time, so nobody can time the go. The flashing speeds up as it gets close, so the go is never a shock — only exactly when it lands.

You win

They moved first

A rising fanfare and a rainbow ring. The screen says they moved first, or they went early! for a false start, and how many seconds into the phase it happened.

You lose

You moved first

Three falling notes and a flashing red ring: you moved first, or you went early. Your opponent's badge saw it, and yours takes its word.

The fine print

What counts as moving

About 20 cm, or any brisk move. When Hold starts, each badge notes where its opponent is. Stray 20 cm from that spot for 0.15 s and that's a move. The radar also feels speed, so a step, a lean or a reach counts the moment it starts, without waiting for the distance; anything over about 30 cm/s for 0.15 s is moving. Breathing and sway sit well under both.

The countdown counts too. Steady is watched exactly like Hold, from the spot you were in when it started. Moving during it is a false start and loses just the same.

Vanishing is not moving. Stand still enough and the radar loses sight of you altogether, which is precisely what the game asks for. The screen opposite then says cannot see them, and you are still in. But a step and then a freeze is a move: vanish after straying the 20 cm and you've lost.

Stepping out of view and back gets you nothing. The badge keeps the spot it first noted and measures your return against it.

The time on the result is how far into the phase the move came: after 7.4s on a Hold, or a fraction of a second on a twitchy false start.

Two rainbows means a draw. If you both move at the same instant, each badge can see the other move first, and both claim the win. Call it a draw and go again.

Fair play

Two rules the radar can't enforce

The badge measures where the person in front of it is, compared with where the badge is pointing. Two things can fool that, and both are house rules.

The badges hold still too

Turn a badge and the person in front of it seems to move. At 3 m, a 4° turn shifts them sideways by 20 cm — the whole threshold. So a badge that swings or twists can hand its own player a win they didn't earn. Stand the badges on something, or hold them flat against your chest. Twisting your badge on purpose loses the round.

Nobody walks between you

Each badge judges the nearest person in front of it. Someone who steps between the players, or closer to a badge than its opponent, becomes the person it's judging, and their walking past is a move. Spectators behind the players or well to the side. If someone walks through, replay the round.

What the screen shows

Your view

The screen faces you, the player holding or standing behind the badge. The radar's fan opens upward from the bottom of the circle: its ±60° view, with arcs 1.5 m apart out to 6 m. The dots round the rim mirror the 24 LEDs on the front.

Across the room

The ring is the referee

Both rings always show the same phase, so each player can see where the round is from their opponent's badge, and so can anyone watching. It never shows anything subtle: a flickering light at the edge of your eye is exactly what makes people twitch, so during Hold it is plain solid blue.

Listen, too

The go is the moment that matters, and you are looking at your opponent, not a light. So it has a sound of its own.

A short tick: the other badge has turned up.

A rising four-note fanfare.

Three falling notes.

Rev F boards have no piezo; one is added by hand on the Dev Module's D32 pin, and later boards have it built in. Without one the game is the same, just silent. Settings → Sound turns it off.

Controls

Buttons and the pad

The three buttons are on the back, under the screen: UP, DOWN, SELECT from left to right as you look at the screen. The touch pad is on the front, across the mantle.

The radio

Badge to badge

The badges talk directly over ESP-NOW, the ESP32's own short-range radio, with a shared key that keeps stray traffic out. No Wi-Fi network and no phone: badges in Duel within a few tens of metres find each other.

Each badge picks one opponent, and a duel needs both to agree. A badge picks a badge that has picked it, or failing that the strongest signal, which is usually the one it's facing. The round starts only once the two have picked each other, and from then on each listens to its opponent alone. A badge waiting its turn nearby, or another duel in range, can't start, stop or decide your round.

When every badge in range is busy, the screen says the others are busy until one comes free. After a round the two players usually pair again for a rematch; a waiting badge gets its turn when one of them leaves Duel, about eight seconds after it goes quiet.

The strongest signal isn't always the person facing you. Check the name after vs. If it's wrong, the badge you're paired with can leave Duel for a moment, or you can, and you'll pair again.

Badges on firmware 1.0.23 or older don't see 1.0.24 badges in Duel: the radio messages changed. Badges update themselves, so this sorts itself out.

Teams. A badge is on the team of the last SAO shape plugged into it (circle, triangle, star or umbrella), and each beacon carries it, so the ready screen shows both. It's for the banter: a team changes nothing about who wins. Badges older than 1.0.28 send no team and show as no team, and duel newer ones as normal.

While in Duel the badge doesn't check for updates or talk to Home Assistant: the radio is busy. Leaving Duel gives it back.

Honest status

Still being tuned

What hasn't been settled yet, most important first.

  • Held badges

    The game was designed for badges held or worn, but a badge that turns makes its opponent appear to move. Standing the badges on something is the fair way to play until there is a better answer, and the house rule above covers the rest.

  • Several duels in one place

    Badges lock on to one opponent for the round, and this has been tested with three badges: two duelling and one waiting beside them, which stayed out of it. Two duels running side by side need four badges, and haven't been tried yet.

  • How still is still enough

    The 20 cm and quarter-second figures come from bench measurement and are shared with Sneak. Duel has been played on two badges facing each other, but not yet by a queue of competitive players.

  • No score

    Each duel stands alone: there are no rounds won, no match and no record. Best of five is kept out loud.
