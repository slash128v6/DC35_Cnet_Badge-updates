Radar Badge Guide

A squid with a 24 GHz radar in its middle. It sees people moving in front of it — how far, and which way — and turns that into ten modes, from party games to a Home Assistant sensor — plus an eleventh that listens to Wi-Fi instead.

Getting oriented

Two faces

The radar and the ring point at the room. The screen and the buttons point at you. The renders show the rev G board as it is made; the drawings under them trace its real outline and name the parts. The panel behind the arms, printed with a reef, is the size of a slim power bank, which tapes to its back.

Power

USB-C, on the Dev Module. Use a good charger or power bank rather than a laptop port on a long cable — Wi-Fi is the most demanding thing the badge does and the first thing to fail on weak power. There is no battery gauge on this board, so no battery figure appears on screen.

The touch pad never touches the menu. It does three things and nothing else, so a knock against a table can't launch a mode or wipe your Wi-Fi credentials.

Hold SELECT to go back works everywhere but one place: during a Sneak round SELECT is the TAG button, and a hard slap can easily last 0.7 s. Holding it does nothing mid-round — press BOOT to leave.

Flipping the screen turns it upside down for when the badge hangs or stands the other way up. The radar picture flips with it, so people still show on the correct side.

Where everything lives

The menu

The badge starts here every time it's switched on, unless Settings → Start says otherwise — and except straight after saving Wi-Fi settings, when it goes back into Home Assistant mode by itself.

Five entries, which is exactly what the round screen shows, so everything is in view at once. Games holds the games, the pet squid and the Theremin and Desk the modes you stand on a table and leave running. Each list remembers where you were, so leaving a game puts you back on it. Holding SELECT goes back a level.

While the menu is up, one dim cyan dot creeps round the ring, so a badge on a table still looks alive without lighting the room.

Sneak · one badge

Red Light, Green Light

Stand it upright on a table facing the room. Players start at a line up to 6 m away and try to reach it and TAG — the bullseye, or SELECT.

Most of the time the badge is blind and players can move. Then it flashes amber, turns red and looks: anyone the radar sees moving is caught, the ring points a white light at them, and they walk back to the start.

Only the referee needs a badge.

Duel · two badges

The first to move loses

Two players a few metres apart, each pointing their badge at the other player. The badges agree a countdown between themselves, both rings go blue, and then nobody may move.

Each badge judges its opponent, never its own wearer. The badge can't feel itself move — it can only watch the person opposite. So the call is always made by the other player's equipment, and a badge never declares itself the loser.

The screen counts how far they have strayed from where they started, against the 200 mm it takes to call it moving.

Result
6 s
Winner rainbow, loser flashing red, with how far into the round it happened.
  • Standing perfectly still makes you vanish from the radar — which is exactly what the game asks for. Disappearing is not losing.
  • Stepping out of view and back buys you nothing. The badge keeps the position it first anchored on.
  • No network needed. The badges talk directly to each other over ESP-NOW, with a shared key that keeps stray traffic out. No Wi-Fi — just both in Duel mode within radio range.
  • One opponent, chosen by both. The screen shows finding and the other badge's name, then vs and the name once the two have picked each other — check it's who you meant. From then on the badge listens to that opponent alone, so other badges nearby can't interfere.
  • Duel and Home Assistant can't run at once. The radio does one or the other; entering Duel takes it and leaving gives it back.
  • With one badge in range the screen says no other badge yet and waits; when every badge in range is already paired, the others are busy.
  • Keep the badges still. A badge that turns makes the person in front of it look as if they moved — at 3 m, a 4° turn is the whole 20 cm. Stand them on something, or hold them flat against your chest.
  • Nobody between the players. Each badge judges the nearest person in front of it.
  • Teams. Once either badge has had an SAO plugged in, the ready screen shows both teams, such as STAR vs CIRCLE (see SAOs). It's for the banter: a team doesn't change who wins.
  • Badges on 1.0.23 or older don't see 1.0.24 badges in Duel — the radio messages changed. They update themselves.

The complete rules, with the screens, the ring and fair play, are in the Duel rulebook.

Boss mode

Eyes in the back of your head

Stand it on your desk facing the room behind you. It ignores everything within 0.9 m — that's you — and warns you when someone within 3.5 m closes at least 30 cm in under a second while heading your way.

Someone walking past, or pacing on the far side of the room, doesn't set it off.

Screen
A flashing red frame around the rim and INCOMING, with the distance and angle. If they stop, it says last seen and standing still? — the radar can lose sight of someone who holds still.
Ring
Dim cyan while watching; a dim, slow amber breath on an alert. The ring faces the person walking up, so it stays subtle on purpose. The loud alert is the screen, which only you can see.
To clear it
SELECT, or a touch on the bullseye — the quiet option, with no reaching round the back of the badge while someone stands over you. The alert stays until you do.
Hold SELECT
During an alert, clears it first; hold again to leave.
Bling → Ring bling

Wear it out

For walking around. The screen dims and the ring's power is capped to stretch the supply.

The ring follows people. Whoever the radar can see lights the part of the ring facing them — warm and bright up close, cool and dim across the room — and the glow moves as they do. With nobody about it drifts through a rainbow instead, so an empty corridor still looks alive.

The row in the pill is the one UP/DOWN changes — SELECT steps through them. There's no other way to tell which a press will affect.

Bling → Flying squids

A screen saver, with squids

A flock of squids flies across the round screen on flapping fins, mantle first, arms streaming behind, with the odd pearl drifting along among them over a twinkling night sea.

It watches the room. When somebody comes within about a metre and a half the flock startles and flies faster, and the ring — facing them — lights warm toward each person the radar sees, over a comet that circles at the flock's pace.

UP and DOWN make the flock bigger or smaller, one to twelve. SELECT changes the colours: PINK, RAINBOW, TEAM (your SAO team's) and GOLD. Hold SELECT to go back.

Every badge in the room, one light show

Badges in Swarm share a clock over the badge-to-badge radio, so their rings pulse, spin and sparkle together. One badge leads: it sets the clock and picks the show, and the rest follow, passing the clock on to badges further away.

Home Assistant mode

A presence sensor

The badge joins your Wi-Fi, connects to your MQTT broker and announces itself. Nothing needs configuring on the Home Assistant side.

Wi-Fi on this board is strong — it joins access points the earlier ideaspark badge refused outright. Anything around −70 dBm or better is comfortable, and the signal is on screen and in Home Assistant as a diagnostic.

The ring breathes cyan while the room is occupied, and sits very dim green when it's clear.

You'll need

  • Home Assistant with the MQTT integration and a broker, such as the Mosquitto add-on — and its IP address, username and password.
  • A 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi network. The badge can't see 5 GHz.
  • A phone or laptop to fill in the setup page.

  1. Pick Home Assistant from the menu.

    The screen says Wi-Fi setup and shows a network name like badge_f9d108-setup and the address 192.168.4.1.

  2. Join that network from your phone.

    It's open, no password. Your phone may warn that it has no internet — stay connected anyway.

  3. Open http://192.168.4.1.

    Plain http. The setup page is named after your badge.

  4. Pick your Wi-Fi network.

    Not listed? Press Rescan. The setup network drops for a few seconds while it scans, so rejoin if your phone wanders off. For a hidden network, type its name in the box underneath.

  5. Fill in the rest.

    Wi-Fi password, the MQTT broker's IP address, port (usually 1883), and the MQTT username and password.

  6. Press Save and restart.

    The badge restarts straight back into Home Assistant mode. The screen shows Wi-Fi..., then MQTT..., then online, and the device appears under Settings → Devices & services → MQTT, named after the badge.

Occupancy turns on fast and off slowly

The radar only sees movement, so someone sitting still at a desk keeps vanishing from it for a few seconds at a time. Occupancy switches on at the first sighting and off only after 60 seconds with nobody seen at all. Build automations around that. Updates go out on every change and at least every 15 s.

When it can't connect

  • Wi-Fi never connected (four tries of 45 s) — back to the setup page, which says Wi-Fi did not connect.
  • The broker rejected the username or password — back to the setup page.
  • The broker couldn't be reached for 2 minutes — back to the setup page.
  • Once it has been online with the saved settings, it never falls back to setup again. A later drop just means it keeps retrying.

To change network or broker later, use Settings → Wi-Fi setup.

Ring clock

A clock on the ring

Both the ring and the screen become a watch dial. An amber hour hand, a green minute hand and a dim blue second hand sweeping round, with faint marks at 12, 3, 6 and 9. Twelve is the top of the ring — the same direction everything else on this badge points.

Reading the ring
The hands sit between LEDs as well as on them, lighting two in proportion, so the minute hand reads to the minute rather than in the 2.5-minute steps 24 LEDs would otherwise allow.
Reading the screen
A dial, with minute dots, thicker marks at the quarters, the same three hands, and a date window at six o'clock carrying the weekday, the date and the time zone. SELECT swaps it for a digital readout — big time, date, and how long ago the clock last synced — and back again. Whichever you leave it on is remembered.
Where the time comes from
The network. The badge has no battery-backed clock — and it never switches Wi-Fi on for this. It takes the time whenever the radio is already up: every ten minutes or so while it checks for updates, and continuously in Home Assistant mode. It asks the moment the radio connects, and keeps it on a few seconds longer if the answer has not arrived yet — so on the defaults it sets itself within about ten seconds of being switched on.
Before it is set
One dot circles the ring instead of a pair of stopped hands — stopped hands look like a fault, and this is not one — and the dial on screen stays empty with NOT SET in its date window. If the screen says clock not set yet, set Wi-Fi up, or, with automatic updates off, use Settings → Updates → Check now to put the radio on long enough to catch the time.
Time zone
On the setup page, the same one as Wi-Fi: a list of common zones plus a box for a POSIX TZ string. Until one is set the badge shows UTC and says so, rather than guessing a zone and being confidently hours wrong. The listed zones carry daylight-saving rules, so the badge changes with the clocks by itself.
Drift
The time survives a restart, an update included, because the chip keeps counting through it — but on a plain crystal with no temperature compensation, so a day off the network leaves it minutes out rather than seconds. The screen says how long ago it synced, in amber once that is over twelve hours.
Alarm
UP or DOWN on the clock opens it. There, UP and DOWN move the time five minutes a press (thirty once held), SELECT turns it on or off, and holding SELECT goes back and saves. It can be set on the setup page too. While it is on, the dial carries an amber dot at the hour it will ring.
When it rings
In any mode — an alarm that only works on one screen is one you miss. It beeps, the ring flashes and the screen says ALARM; whatever you were doing waits underneath. SELECT or the touch pad snoozes it for 9 minutes, BOOT turns it off, and it gives up by itself after two minutes. With Sound off it flashes but stays quiet. It needs the clock to be set, and says so when it is not.
Brightness
Dimmer than any other mode on purpose. This is the one that sits on a desk for hours, possibly in the dark.

Set Settings → Start to CLOCK and the badge comes up as a clock after a power cut, which is the point of a desk clock.

Wi-Fi watch

Listens for Wi-Fi attacks

The badge listens to the Wi-Fi around it — every 2.4 GHz channel in turn, a sweep every few seconds — and raises the alarm on the attacks a hacker con actually sees. It only ever listens: it joins nothing and transmits nothing, so it can't disturb anyone's network.

Two alerts. Someone is flooding channel 6 with disconnect frames, and a network called IoT-Lab is being broadcast both open and with a password. The ring breathes red until things have been quiet for 30 seconds.

Deauth flood
A burst of "disconnect" frames on one channel, seen twice within ten seconds. Knocking people off a network is how an attacker makes their devices reconnect — to a copy of the network, or so the reconnection can be captured. A single burst on its own is ignored: access points kick clients off every so often, and repeat themselves when they do.
Twin
One network name broadcast both open and protected. The classic "evil twin" copies a real network's name without its password and waits for devices to join it. The badge knows no network names in advance; it compares what it hears with itself.
Many names
One access point announcing four or more different network names. Real access points give each network its own address; a "Karma" rogue answers to whatever name a phone asks for, all from one radio.
New AP (once you pin)
Pin your own network and the badge remembers every access point broadcasting it at that moment. From then on, that name from any other access point — or one of yours changing its security — is an alert. This catches the twin the check above can't: one that copied the password as well, which at a con is often printed on the wall.
Pinning
Do it somewhere you trust, with your network in range: on the NETWORKS page press SELECT, move to your network with UP/DOWN and press SELECT again. The WATCH page then says guarding and the name, and in the network list your access points show in cyan and any stranger using the name in red. Choose the pinned network again to unpin it — after adding an access point at home, say — and pin it once more. One network at a time; Reset clears it.
Pages
UP/DOWN step through three, as does SELECT except on NETWORKS, where it picks a network to pin. WATCH: live alerts in red, marked NOW. Once things have been quiet for 30 seconds it says QUIET, with the last alert underneath under EARLIER so you can still see what happened. NETWORKS: what is nearby, strongest first, with signal in dBm, channel, security and name; open networks in amber. CHANNELS: how busy each channel is, with a red bar where a flood is going on.
The ring
Quiet: one dim green dot stepping round as the badge moves from channel to channel. Alert: the whole ring breathes red, and the piezo sounds once for each new alert.
The radio
Wi-Fi watch has it to itself, as Duel and the CTF do: no update checks and no Home Assistant while it runs. Leaving switches the radio off. The ESP32 only hears 2.4 GHz, so 5 GHz networks and attacks on them don't appear.

What it can't tell you. An alert means the pattern is on the air, not who is behind it or that anyone is the target. Some venues' own access points flood deauths at networks they consider rogue, and some phone hotspots share a name with a real network by accident. It is a smoke alarm, not a verdict.

Desk → SAOs

Collect the shapes, pick a team

The badge recognises the four SAOs of its set (a circle, a triangle, a star and an umbrella) by one resistor on each, and remembers every shape it has had plugged in. Each SAO kit has one shape, so the rest come from trading and borrowing.

Three of four, on team Star. A shape you haven't had is a grey outline. Once you have, it's filled in its colour, and it's circled while it's plugged in.

The ring shows the same, a quarter per shape from the top: cyan, red, amber, magenta. The plugged-in one pulses.

The shapes
Circle, a pearl, cyan. Triangle, a shark fin, red. Star, a sea star, amber. Umbrella, a jellyfish, magenta. The SAO assembly guide builds them.
Your team
The shape of the last SAO you plugged in. It stays when you unplug it; plug in another to change. Duel shows both players' teams, and the CTF leaderboard marks each badge's team and adds the teams up.
All four
Having had every shape plugged in, one at a time, captures the CTF's Shape hunt flag, 100 points.
Checking
Every two seconds, twice a second on this screen, and never while the CTF is open, which uses those pins itself. An SAO from outside the set reads as an SAO this badge doesn't know, and is harmless. Over USB serial, SAO names what is plugged in, the collection and the team.
Desk → SAO bench

The SAO port as a bench tool

Four tools on the SAO header, for hacking on an SAO or anything else that runs at 3.3 V. Pick one with UP, DOWN and SELECT; hold SELECT to go back to the list.

3.3 V logic only

Nothing here level-shifts: 5 V into these pins will damage the badge. The header's pins are 1 3V3, 2 GND, 3 SDA, 4 SCL, 5 and 6 the two GPIOs.

I2C scan
Every address that answers on SDA/SCL, with a guess at the common ones: OLED, EEPROM, IMU, BME280 and so on. SELECT scans again. A line held low is reported rather than left to hang the scan.
Logic probe
The level on pins 5, 6, SDA and SCL, and the frequency on pins 5 and 6, averaged over a second, up to 300 kHz. The chip's pulse counters do the counting, so a fast signal doesn't slow the badge. SELECT changes the pull resistor: down, up or none.
Signal out
On pin 5. SELECT steps through off, square (1 Hz to 100 kHz), sine (200 Hz to 100 kHz, from the chip's own DAC) and DC (0 to 3.3 V). UP and DOWN change the frequency or the voltage.
UART bridge
Joins the USB serial console to the SAO pins: the badge transmits on pin 5 and receives on pin 6. UP and DOWN set the baud rate, 9600 to 230400; SELECT starts and stops it. While it runs, what you type goes to the device, not to the badge's own commands, and the screen shows the byte counts and the latest text received.
Afterwards
The pins go back to plain inputs whenever a tool closes, and the badge doesn't check which SAO is plugged in while the bench is open.
Squid

Keep the squid off the coral

The squid swims by itself through a porthole of sea. Coral grows up from the sand and hangs down from above, with a gap to get through; jellyfish drift in the open water between. Three lives, and it gets faster and tighter the further you go.

The squid keeps to its column on the left and the sea comes to it: coral from above and below with a gap to get through, a jellyfish in the open water after it, and a pearl beyond that.

Under the score, the pink pips are lives and the dark pips are ink. Here one life has gone and one shot of ink is on its way to the jellyfish.

The title screen has the controls, your best score and the screen-speed setting.

Pearls
Points, and one shot of ink back.
A crowd

Kept when the badge is switched off. The course is the same every run, so it means something.
Music
A bouncy tune while you swim, quickening as the course does. Settings → Sound set to EFFECTS keeps the effects without it.
Screen speed

The badge talks to its screen at 40 MHz, which gives the game its full 30 frames a second. The title screen shows the speed. To try another, press UP: the badge restarts at the next speed (80, then 20) for one boot only. If the picture is clean, DOWN keeps it, for every mode; if it is speckled or blank, switch off and on and nothing was saved. If the screen is ever speckled or garbled in every mode, hold UP and DOWN together while switching on: that sets the safe 20 MHz, which every badge runs, without needing to read the screen.

Games → Squid Rush

A high-speed platformer, starring a squid

Run, roll and jet through three zones — Coral Reef, Kelp Forest and the Trench — collecting pearls, bouncing off coral springs and curling into a ball to bowl over crabs, jellyfish and rival ink-squids. At the bottom of the Trench waits the Kraken.

DOWN held runs right, UP held runs left. SELECT jumps — hold it for a higher one — and curls you into a ball that bowls over anything it lands on. Touch the pad on the top edge to ink-jet: a burst of speed, rolled up and unstoppable, every second and a half.

Hills are momentum. Run down one and you carry the speed up the next and off its lip; launch ramps throw you over trenches. Too slow to climb? Back up for a run at it, or ink-jet.

Pearls
Your shield. Hit something while you hold pearls and they scatter — grab what you can before they sink. Hit with none and it's a life. Three lives, and a checkpoint buoy halfway through each zone.
The tally
Pays for pearls and for speed, and — as in Squid — every person the radar sees watching multiplies it, up to four times.
Ink-squids
Hover, drift toward you and spit ink. One swells and darkens just before it fires; the ink hurts unless you're ink-jetting through it. Bowl them over like anything else.
The Kraken
Lurks behind the far cliff of its arena. It raises a tentacle over wherever you stand — a shadow on the floor says where — and slams it down, so keep moving. After a few slams it lowers its great head to glare at you: that's the opening. Jump and come down on it in a ball. Six hits; it gets quicker as it hurts, and from halfway it spits ink too.
Ring
The speedometer, green to yellow to red; gold for a pearl, red for a hit, and the Kraken's health during the fight.
Music
A tune for each zone and one for the Kraken, all written for the badge. The squid wears your SAO team's colour, and the best score is kept.

The red dots on the rim are squids behind you, brighter as they close. The green bar under the score is your health.

Back to the menu. Holding SELECT only works on the title and "eaten" screens: mid-game it is the fire button.
Behind you
No walking — three buttons can't turn, walk and fire — so you stand your ground. A squid you can't see is a red dot on the rim of the screen at its bearing, brighter as it closes, and a bite flashes the rim on the side it came from.
Health
The bar under the score. A squid that reaches you bites every second or so until you ink it, and they come faster the longer you last.
The ring
For onlookers: a red light toward each squid, a flash on the side of a bite, a flicker for every shot.
Music
A driving riff that quickens as squids fill the hall, and again when your health is low. Settings → Sound set to EFFECTS keeps the effects without it.
Real people are cyan squids

Hold the badge to play and its radar faces the way you do, so a real person moving in front of you appears in the game as a cyan squid at their real bearing. They are harmless, and inking one is worth more than a normal squid — though each person can only be tagged once every few seconds.

It redraws the whole screen every frame, so its full 30 frames a second needs the screen at 40 MHz, as it is out of the box. It is the Wolfenstein technique rather than Doom itself: real Doom needs memory this badge does not have. The best score is kept when the badge is switched off.

Games → Squidstack

Falling squids, stacked into lines

Every piece is a squid — a head with eyes and three arms — in one of seven poses. Fill a row from wall to wall and it bursts; let the heap reach the top and you're inked out. A squid's arms stay joined as it lands, so the stack is a heap of squids, not a wall of bricks.

UP and DOWN move it left and right (hold to slide), SELECT turns it, and holding UP and DOWN together drops it faster. Touch the pad on the top edge to slam it straight down. BOOT goes back to the menu; holding SELECT only does on the title and game-over screens.

A faint outline shows where it will land, and NEXT shows the one after. One to four rows at once score 100, 300, 500 or 800, times the level; every ten rows the squids fall faster and the music quickens.

The crowd
As in Squid, the radar is in the game: each person watching the badge adds a multiple to a line's points, up to four times, shown as crowd beside the well.
Ring
Fills toward the next level in the colour of the falling squid, flashes white on a clear, and runs a rainbow for four rows at once.
Music
"Deep Polka", a quick folk dance in A minor, written for the badge. Settings → Sound set to EFFECTS keeps the effects without it.
Best score
Kept when the badge is switched off.
Games → Pet squid

A squid to raise

The first visit adopts an egg. From then on it lives whenever the badge is on, whatever mode you're in, and it's kept when the badge is switched off — switched off, it sleeps, and neither ages nor gets hungry.

Three meters along the top run down by themselves: food (the pearl) in about ten hours, fun (the heart) in about seven, and clean (the bubble) drops as it leaves ink blots on the sand, one every hour and a quarter. A line under them says what it wants.

UP and DOWN choose along the bottom and SELECT does it: FEED drops it a pearl, PLAY is a guessing game — will it jet UP or DOWN? five rounds — CLEAN washes the blots away, and INFO shows its age, when it next grows, and its meals, games, baths and friends. Hold SELECT to go back.

Its eyes follow the people the radar sees. The ring breathes in its colour, sparkles when it eats or grows, and goes dim blue while it sleeps.

Growing up
An egg for a couple of minutes, a baby for its first two hours, a kid until eight hours old, then a grown-up, which takes the colour of your SAO team and grows spots once it has met ten other badges. Raising it to a grown-up captures the CTF's Squidling flag.
Fun
Besides PLAY, it enjoys company — people near the badge while its screen is open — and hearing what you've been up to: a run of Squid or Squoom, a Duel won, a CTF flag, a new badge on the CTF board.
Sick
Left with nothing to eat, or with three blots about, for an hour, it turns green, sweats and stops growing until it's better. Feeding it to half and cleaning up cures it. It never dies — this is a con badge.
Night
When the badge knows the time (it has Wi-Fi), it sleeps from 11 pm to 7 am and its meters run down at half speed. Opening its screen wakes it for a few minutes.
Playdates
While its screen is open, the pet calls out to other badges. Hold two badges with their pets open close together — about a metre — and the other squid swims over to play: hearts, both cheer up, and each new squid it meets counts as a friend. Its screen also carries Ink outbreak.
Serial
PET says how it's doing.
Games → Theremin

Play the badge by moving

Stand the badge up facing the room and step in front of it. Closer is higher: two octaves, from C5 at about 2.7 m to C7 at 30 cm. Move off to one side for vibrato — the further out, the more. Two or three people at once play an arpeggio of their notes.

UP and DOWN choose the scale: SLIDE (no steps at all, a real theremin), PENTATONIC, MAJOR, BLUES or CHROMATIC. SELECT mutes and unmutes; hold it to go back.

The screen shows the note, its frequency and your distance, the waveform, and a keyboard with your note lit and the notes outside the scale marked. The ring faces the players: it glows in the colour of the note, and each player's LED points at them.

Keep moving

The radar only sees people who move, so when you stand still the note fades after a moment — sway a little. The note comes from the piezo, so Settings → Sound has to be on.

Games → Compose

Write a tune, send it round

Sixteen steps loop round, each a rest or one of eleven notes of A minor pentatonic over two octaves — a scale where every note sounds right with every other, so anything you write is a tune. It plays while you write.

Writing
UP and DOWN change the note on this step; SELECT moves on to the next. Hold SELECT to go back — your tune is kept.
Sending
Touch the pad on the top edge to send it to every badge nearby with Compose open. A tune sent to you plays straight away, with who sent it; SELECT while it plays keeps it in place of yours, or leave it and yours comes back.
Ring
The sixteen steps round the ring, the playing one lit. Settings → Sound has to be on; ALL and EFFECTS both play it.
Games → Outbreak

Ink spreads, badge to badge

A con-wide infection game. A few badges start out as patient zero. An inked badge puffs ink at the badges around it, and a clean badge that stays close to an inked one for a few seconds is inked too — and passes it on. Nobody gets cured; it's ink.

Where it spreads
Whenever two badges both have the badge-to-badge radio up: Outbreak, Swarm, the pet squid and Compose all carry it.
The screen
Whether you're inked, whose outbreak it is, who inked you, how many badges you've inked yourself, and how long the longest chain has grown. A clean badge flashes purple, ring and screen, while someone is inking it.
Patient zero
The first time a badge opens Outbreak it has a one-in-twelve chance of being one. Over USB serial, INK says how it stands and INK ZERO makes a clean badge a patient zero — for whoever is running the con.
CTF

Capture the flag

Ten challenges, 900 points between them, and a leaderboard that spreads from badge to badge — no server, no Wi-Fi. For bragging rights.

Capturing
Some flags are automatic: the badge sees you open the radar lock, reach a score, win a Duel, plug in the last of the four SAO shapes, have 25 other badges on your leaderboard, or raise your pet squid. The rest end in an 8-character code — letters and digits, no I, O, 0 or 1. Enter it under Enter code (UP/DOWN letter, SELECT next, hold SELECT to try it), or as FLAG <code> over USB serial.
Per badge
Codes are different on every badge, so telling a friend yours won't help them. Taking the firmware apart to work out how they are made is fair game.
Radar lock
Stand the badge facing the room, open Radar lock and walk out in front. The ring shows where the radar sees you: blue is cold, red is hot, green and filling is right. Stay about three seconds and it clicks; three places open it.
Leaderboard
While the CTF is open the badge swaps scores with nearby badges that have it open too, and passes on what it has heard, so the board builds up as people cross paths. Your row is green, and each badge's SAO team is a small shape before its name. It shares only your badge name, score and team, and it is kept when the badge is switched off.
Teams
SELECT on the leaderboard shows every team's points added up, best first, and how many badges are on each. A badge drops entries it can't make sense of, such as flags or teams its firmware doesn't know, so badges on older firmware leave newer ones off their board until they update themselves. Newer badges list the older ones, without a team.
While it's open
No update checks (the radio is busy with the leaderboard), and SAO pin 5 and the SAO's I²C pins are in use — take off an add-on that uses them first.
Settings

Make it yours

Set name
Up to 8 characters: A–Z, 0–9, space and dash. UP/DOWN change the letter, SELECT moves to the next, and holding SELECT finishes — leaving always saves. The name shows in the menu and Bling mode, and names the device in Home Assistant.
Wi-Fi setup
Confirms, then forgets the saved network and opens the setup page, where you enter the network the badge should join. The same page carries the optional MQTT details, an Update URL box — leave that blank, which means "use the built-in one" — and the same automatic-updates and start-up choices as the rows below — so a badge can be set up and told to come up as a presence sensor in one go — plus the time zone the ring clock reads. The URL box is there so a badge can be pointed at a different update server without being reflashed.
Sound: ALL / EFFECTS / OFF
SELECT steps through the three. ALL is everything, including the background music in Squid and Squoom, which plays only while a game is on and speeds up as it gets harder. EFFECTS keeps the effects without the music. OFF is silent, for a talk. Sound needs a piezo: rev G boards have one built in, and on rev F one is added by hand to the Dev Module's D32 pin; without one this does nothing either way. With one fitted: effects throughout Squid and Squoom; Sneak calls go, warns before it turns and buzzes a catch; Duel ticks, says go and plays a win or a loss; Boss rings a soft doorbell when someone walks up; the menu clicks.
Start: MENU / BOSS / SNEAK / BLING / HASS / CLOCK
Which mode the badge comes up in when switched on. SELECT steps through the six and wraps; the row shows the current one, and it survives a power cycle and an update. MENU is the default. The others skip it — a badge set to HASS goes straight back to being a presence sensor after a power cut, which is the whole point; BLING suits wearing it around, BOSS and CLOCK leaving it on a desk. Holding SELECT or pressing BOOT still returns to the menu, so this cannot lock you out. Two deliberate gaps: Duel is not offered, because it takes the radio to talk to the other badge and a badge booting into it would never check for updates; and HASS falls back to the menu with no Wi-Fi stored, so a badge that has been reset comes up somewhere recognisable rather than in a captive portal.
Updates
A small list of its own. Check now looks for new firmware straight away, whatever the next row says. Automatic: ON / OFF is whether the badge looks by itself: ON, the default, checks every ten minutes or so and installs what it finds; OFF stops it checking — and, with nothing else wanting the network, stops it switching the Wi-Fi radio on at all. Updates still work with Check now. Worth choosing if you want to know exactly which firmware is on the badge during a talk, or would rather it stayed off the network you are on. See Updates.
Reset
Confirms, then wipes the name, Wi-Fi, MQTT and Bling settings and restarts. Settings survive firmware updates, so reset before handing the badge on, or your network goes with it.

Seven rows, so this list scrolls a little — Reset and Back sit past the bottom of the first screenful. Holding SELECT goes back from anywhere, which is usually quicker. The firmware version is shown at the top.

Updates

It updates itself

Given a Wi-Fi network, the badge fetches and installs its own new firmware. The version it is running is at the top of the Settings screen, so you can tell at a glance whether one arrived.

When it looks
Shortly after switching on, then roughly every ten minutes. Settings → Updates → Check now asks straight away.
Turning it off
Set Settings → Updates → Automatic to OFF and it stops looking — and stops bringing the radio up by itself. Check now still works, so that is "ask me", not "never".
What you see
Progress on the screen while it downloads, then it restarts into the new version.
What it needs
A network, set up once through Settings → Wi-Fi setup. Without one it never looks.
What it keeps
Everything. Your name, your Wi-Fi details and your Bling settings all survive. Only Reset clears those.
It will not install firmware from anyone else

Every update is signed, and the badge checks that signature against a key built into it before it writes anything. An update not signed with that key is refused — so being on a stranger's Wi-Fi does not let someone push their own firmware onto your badge. It also checks the downloaded file against a fingerprint inside the signed notice, so a file that arrives damaged or altered is thrown away rather than installed.

When it can't update itself: install by USB

A badge on firmware 1.0.20 or older looks for updates at an address that no longer serves them; a badge with no Wi-Fi never looks; and one that won't start can't. All three take one install over a USB cable, and then update over Wi-Fi again. Your name, Wi-Fi details, settings, scores and CTF progress are kept unless you choose to erase.

  1. From your browser — the easy way. Open Flash from your browser in Chrome or Edge on a computer, plug the badge in with a data cable, and click Connect and install. The page checks the release's signature first, exactly as the badge would, and installs nothing it can't verify. Leave Erase device unticked to keep your settings.
  2. With the cable kit — for other browsers, or no browser. Download the updates repository (Code → Download ZIP) and unzip it. If you don't have the Arduino IDE or Python, get Espressif's standalone esptool: from esptool's releases page download esptool-v…-windows-amd64.zip and unzip it into the kit's flash folder. Then double-click flash\flash.cmd. The kit's instructions cover macOS and Linux too.

No badge in the list, or no COM port? The computer needs the driver for the board's USB chip — a Silicon Labs CP210x on these badges, a CH340 on some other ESP32 boards. On Windows, open Device Manager: the badge should be under Ports (COM & LPT). If it isn't, or shows under Other devices with a warning sign, install the CP210x Universal Windows Driver from Silicon Labs (or CH341SER for a CH340 board), then plug the badge in again. macOS usually needs nothing; Linux never does, though you may need to join the dialout group. And try another cable first: many only carry power.

A failed update leaves the badge alone. New firmware is written to a spare area, and the badge only switches across once the whole download has passed its checks. If the download fails, or a check doesn't pass, it simply carries on with what it has. And if a new version turns out to crash in its first half-minute, the badge goes back to the previous one by itself.

Two things that look wrong and aren't

  • Up to date just after a new version came out. The update server holds its answer for a few minutes, so a badge that asks too early is told there is nothing new. Wait a little and check again.
  • No checking during a Duel. A duel uses the radio to talk to the other badge, so the badge won't interrupt a game to look for updates — it catches up afterwards. The same goes for while the setup page is open.
No radar attached?

Demo mode

If the badge hears nothing from its radar in the first 3 seconds after switching on, it shows NO RADAR - DEMO and runs Sneak and Boss on simulated people, so you can see how they work. It decides once, at startup: connect a radar afterwards and you'll need to restart.

Home Assistant mode never sends simulated data, and Duel has nothing to simulate — it needs a real opponent badge.

radar idle in the footer means the radar is connected but hasn't reported anyone for 20 s — usually just an empty room.

Troubleshooting

ProblemTry
Can't see the -setup networkWait 10 s, or leave and re-enter Home Assistant mode. It drops briefly during a scan, which Rescan triggers. Stand within a few metres.
Setup page won't loadCheck the phone is still on the badge's network — phones like to hop back to their usual Wi-Fi — then open http://192.168.4.1.
My network isn't listedIt must be 2.4 GHz. Press Rescan, or type the name into the hidden-network box.
Stuck on Wi-Fi...Check the password, use a stronger USB supply, and move nearer an access point.
Stuck on MQTT... or failedCheck the broker's IP, port, username and password, and that the broker accepts the connection.
Occupancy stays on after I leaveExpected for 60 s after the last movement.
Duel says no other badge yetBoth badges must be in Duel mode and within radio range. A badge in Home Assistant mode can't also be duelling.
Duel never calls anyone outPoint each badge at the other player, not at yourself, and stand within about 5 m of each other.
The bullseye does nothingIt only works in Sneak, Duel and Boss — by design. If it's dead there too, check nothing conductive is taped across the mantle.
Screen is upside downHold BOOT for about a second.
NO RADAR - DEMO with a radar fittedCheck the radar's pigtail and power, then restart.
SELECT won't quit SneakOn purpose, mid-round. Press BOOT.
No battery percentageExpected. This board has no battery gauge.
Says Up to date but there is a newer versionThe update server holds its answer for a few minutes. Wait, then Check now again.
Never updates on its ownCheck Settings → Updates → Automatic says ON. It also needs Wi-Fi set up, and it won't interrupt a Duel or the setup page.
I want it to stay off this networkSet Settings → Updates → Automatic to OFF. With Home Assistant mode not running, the badge then never switches the radio on by itself.
Set to start in HASS, but shows the menuStarting there needs Wi-Fi stored, and it falls back to the menu when there is none — after a Reset, for instance. Set Wi-Fi up again.
Starts in a mode and I want the menuHold SELECT, or press BOOT. To change it for good, Settings → Start until it reads MENU.
No sound at allRev F boards have no piezo; one has to be added to the D32 pin by hand. With one fitted, check Settings → Sound says ON.
Screen is speckled or garbled in every modeThe screen is running faster than this badge's wiring can carry. Hold UP and DOWN together while switching on: that sets the safe 20 MHz.
Clock says clock not set yetIt has not been online since switching on. Set Wi-Fi up, or use Updates → Check now to put the radio on briefly.
The alarm did not go offIt has to be on (amber dot on the dial), and the clock has to be set — the alarm screen says if it is not. With Sound off it only flashes.
Clock is out by whole hoursNo time zone set, so it is showing UTC — and saying so under the time. Set one on the setup page.
Bad signature while updatingNothing was installed; the badge is unharmed. In the few minutes after a new release this is usually the update server part-way through switching over — wait five minutes and check again. If it persists, the badge has refused firmware that was not signed for it, which is it working as designed.
Bling ring lights but never movesIt should follow you. If it sits still, the radar isn't being read — restart, and check the radar pigtail if NO RADAR appears.
